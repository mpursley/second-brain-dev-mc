Reguarding Co-Founder/COO role... When negotiating, could I use a contract or agreement to sign on the applicant and award the equity, only if they (we) are able to acquire the funding in the pre-seed round first?


Yes, you can absolutely structure an agreement so that the incoming COO's equity is contingent upon successfully securing the pre-seed funding. Since a primary expectation for this role is to "Lead our Pre-Seed raise", tying their compensation directly to that outcome is a logical approach.


Here are three common ways to execute this based on your current documentation:


1. Rely on the 1-Year Cliff (The Standard Approach)
Your current documents already provide a strong built-in protection mechanism. Both the job description and the deal terms specify a standard 4-year vesting schedule with a 1-year cliff.


If you hire the COO and they fail to close the $1M via YC Post-Money SAFEs within their first 12 months, you can terminate the relationship before the cliff hits.


Under these terms, they would walk away with 0% equity, effectively making the equity contingent on their first-year performance (which is dominated by fundraising).


2. Milestone-Based Vesting
Instead of relying strictly on time-based vesting, you can structure their equity grant—likely pulled from your 2,000,000 share Unallocated Option Pool—to include performance milestones.


You can explicitly write into the grant agreement that a specific percentage of their equity only vests upon the successful closing of the Tier 1 SAFE ($500k) or Tier 2 SAFE ($500k).


Note: You must define the milestone precisely to avoid disputes (e.g., what happens if they raise $400k instead of $500k, or if you, the CEO, actually bring in the lead investor).


3. "Fundraising Consultant" to Co-Founder Transition Agreement
Because the role is advertised as "Equity-only until we close our initial funding round", you can start the relationship with a milestone-based consulting agreement rather than an immediate Co-Founder equity grant.


The contract would stipulate that their sole initial deliverable is securing the pre-seed funding.


The agreement would include a binding clause stating that upon the successful deposit of the target funds into the company's bank account, their status converts to a full-time W-2 Co-Founder, unlocking their target $200k – $225k base salary and triggering their 1.5% – 3.0% equity grant.


While milestone-based contracts offer strict protection, the standard 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff is generally preferred in startups because it requires less complex legal drafting and clearly aligns the founder's long-term incentives with the company's survival.

Delaware Amendment: To issue more than the 10,000,000 shares currently authorized in your Certificate of Incorporation, you must file an "Amendment to the Certificate of Incorporation" with the state of Delaware. This costs legal/filing fees and requires board and stockholder consent.

Dilution Mechanics: You will eventually authorize more shares anyway when you do a Priced Equity Round (Series Seed or Series A) to create the "preferred parity shares" that investors get when their SAFEs convert. What type of restrictions, requirements, etc should we expect the first level of investors require?


Because your capitalization table outlines a strategy to raise your initial $1M using standard YC Post-Money SAFEs, the restrictions and requirements at this first level of funding will be relatively founder-friendly. SAFEs (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) are intentionally designed to defer the heavy legal restrictions until your first priced equity round (like a Series A).


However, pre-seed and seed investors will still have strict fundamental requirements to protect their capital. Here is what you should expect from this first level of investors:


1. Structural & Legal Prerequisites (Already Addressed)
Sophisticated early-stage investors have a rigid checklist of corporate hygiene before they will wire funds. Fortunately, your current setup has already preemptively solved the major ones:


Delaware C-Corporation: Investors almost exclusively require this entity type for tax and structural reasons.


Founder Vesting: Investors require founders to earn their equity over time to ensure they don't leave the company with a massive chunk of dead equity. Your established 4-year vesting schedule with a 1-year cliff satisfies this perfectly.


Clean IP Assignment: Investors need absolute certainty that the company owns its underlying technology. Your Confidential Information and Invention Assignment Agreements (CIIAA) assigning all codebases and domains to the corporation is a critical requirement.


2. Standard SAFE Mechanics
By using YC Post-Money SAFEs, the legal relationship is governed by a few core parameters:


Valuation Caps: Your investors are securing equity at the specific $4M and $8M valuation caps. The primary restriction here is mathematical: when you eventually raise a priced round, these SAFEs will convert into preferred stock at whichever price gives the investors the better deal (the cap or the new round's price).


No Immediate Voting Rights: SAFE holders do not typically receive voting shares immediately. They are essentially holding a warrant for future equity.


3. Common "Side Letter" Requests
While the YC SAFE is a standard template, larger angel investors or early-stage funds (e.g., those writing $250k+ checks) will often ask you to sign a "Side Letter" alongside the SAFE. These typically include:


Pro-Rata Rights (Participation Rights): The right to invest additional capital in your next funding round to maintain their ownership percentage. (YC highly recommends keeping a tight leash on who gets these, usually reserving them only for major lead investors).


Information Rights: A contractual requirement that you provide them with standard financial updates, usually in the form of quarterly income statements, balance sheets, and a brief operational update.


MFN (Most Favored Nation) Clause: If an investor signs a SAFE now, and you issue a SAFE to a different investor later with better terms (e.g., a lower valuation cap), the MFN clause guarantees the first investor automatically gets those better terms.


4. Operational & Financial Expectations
While not explicitly written into a SAFE, pre-seed investors have strict unwritten expectations regarding the use of their funds:


Full-Time Commitment: They expect all key co-founders (CEO, COO, Head of Product) to be fully dedicated to the company.


Lean Founder Salaries: They expect the capital to be used primarily for product development, computing infrastructure, and go-to-market execution. They will expect your C-suite salaries to remain constrained (e.g., the $130k–$165k range) until the company demonstrates significant revenue or raises a much larger Series A.


18-24 Month Runway Target: The explicit expectation for a $1M pre-seed raise is that the capital will buy the company 18 to 24 months of operational runway to hit the necessary metrics for a Series A.


What They Will NOT Usually Require (Yet)
At the SAFE stage, you generally will not be required to:


Give up board seats or board control.


Issue complex protective provisions (where investors can veto your operational decisions).


Pay dividends.


Those heavy restrictions will be negotiated when your SAFEs convert during a future Priced Equity Round.

Even though the candidates will have signed an NDA, it is generally better to send a curated subset of the slides for the initial introductory call.


Because you are hiring this person to lead your pre-seed raise and build the company, you absolutely want them to see the whole picture eventually. However, sending all 26 slides right away can distract from the goal of the first interview.


If you send the Cap Table, P&L, and Data Room indexes, the candidate might spend the 30-minute call debating granular financial modeling or equity splits rather than discussing the high-level Go-To-Market strategy, product vision, and how they plan to raise the $1M.


Here is the recommended approach for what to include and what to hold back for the first round:


Slides to INCLUDE (The "Read-Ahead" Deck)
Send them the core narrative, the product vision, the GTM strategy, and what you are asking investors for. This gives them exactly what they need to evaluate the business and prepare for a strategic conversation.


Slides 1 - 4: Intro, Problem, Solution, Why Now (Sets the stage)


Slides 5 - 9: Market Size, Product Ecosystem, Assistant PhD, Creative Suite, Genius Suite (Shows what they are selling)


Slide 10 & 20: Go-To-Market Strategy (Crucial for a COO to review and critique)


Slide 11 - 13: Competitive Advantage, Traction, Business Models


Slide 14 & 16: Financial Projections (High-level) & The Ask ($1M Pre-Seed)


Slide 15: The Team


Slides 22 - 26: Early-Stage Launch Pages / Screenshots (Gives them a tangible feel for what is already built)


Slides to HOLD BACK (Save for Round 2)
Keep these heavily operational and "due diligence" slides in your back pocket. If the first call goes well, you can use these documents as the basis for a working session in the second interview.


Slide 17 (Capitalization Table): You want to understand their high-level compensation expectations first before anchoring them to your exact pro forma modeling.


Slides 18 & 19 (Data Room Index 1 & 2): These are meant for investors actively auditing your company. They add unnecessary bulk for an interview read-ahead.


Slide 21 (P&L Statement Forecast): While Slide 14 gives the high-level revenue goals, this granular month-by-month breakdown is better saved for a second interview where you ask them to critique your COGS and OpEx assumptions.


How to frame it when sending:
When you send the NDA and the deck, you can say something like:

This sets clear expectations and gives them a specific "homework" assignment for the call.

Yes, there are massive regulatory restrictions you must navigate here. This is one of the most common legal minefields for early-stage founders.


Because you are raising capital via YC SAFEs—which are legally classified as securities—paying someone to help you sell those securities triggers strict federal and state regulations.


Here is the breakdown of the restrictions and how to structure the role to protect the company.


The Broker-Dealer Trap
In the United States (under SEC and FINRA rules), anyone who receives transaction-based compensation (i.e., a success fee or commission percentage based on the amount of capital raised) for helping to sell securities must be registered as a Broker-Dealer.


If your Strategic Advisor is not a licensed Broker-Dealer, paying them a 5% cash commission for closing the round is illegal under federal securities law.


The Risks to OpenWorldTech
If you use an unregistered broker to raise your pre-seed round, the consequences for the company can be severe:


The Right of Rescission: This is the biggest danger. If investors find out an unregistered broker was used, they legally have the "right of rescission." This means that if the company hits a rough patch in a year, those investors can demand their full $500k or $1M back immediately, effectively bankrupting the startup.


Poisoning Future Rounds: When you eventually go to raise a Series A, institutional venture capital firms will perform deep legal due diligence. If they see an illegal commission paid to an unregistered finder on your books, they will often walk away from the deal entirely because the cap table is considered "toxic" with rescission risk.


The "Finder" Myth
You will often hear people in the startup world say they are just a "Finder" who makes introductions. There is a very narrow legal exemption for true Finders, but it almost never applies if the person is:


Actively pitching the company to investors.


Negotiating the terms of the SAFEs.


Giving advice on the valuation caps ($4M or $8M).


Getting paid only if the deal closes.


How to Structure the Deal Legally
To hire an executive like Mark (or someone in his network) without running afoul of the SEC, you have a few compliant paths:


1. Verify Their Status:
If they want a 5% cash success fee, simply ask if they are a registered Broker-Dealer (many senior financial executives actually carry these FINRA licenses, like Series 7 or Series 82). If they are, you are perfectly fine to pay the commission.

That is a fantastic question, and it cuts right to the heart of how early-stage startups legally navigate fundraising.


The short answer is no, Advisory Board members do not need FINRA or SEC licensing, but only if their compensation and day-to-day activities are structured correctly.


The SEC’s requirement for a broker-dealer license is triggered by what a person does and how they are paid. Here is exactly how to keep your Advisory Board compliant and avoid crossing the line into unregistered broker-dealer territory.


1. The Golden Rule: No Transaction-Based Compensation


The absolute biggest red flag for the SEC is transaction-based compensation. This means paying someone (in cash or equity) contingent upon the successful closing of a capital raise, or scaling their pay based on the amount raised.


The Compliant Way: Your advisor's 0.2% to 1.0% equity grant must vest based on time or service, not fundraising success. For example, the equity vests monthly over 24 months in exchange for a commitment of 2 to 4 hours of strategic advising per month.


The Illegal Way: Giving an advisor a 1.0% equity grant that only vests if they successfully introduce you to an investor who writes a check. That is a success fee in disguise, which requires a FINRA license.


2. The Limits of Their Role (What They Can and Cannot Do)


Even if they are paid legally via time-vested equity, you must ensure their actions don't inadvertently act as a broker.


What an Unlicensed Advisor CAN Do:


Provide Warm Introductions: They can look at their Rolodex and say, "I know a partner at X Ventures who loves this space. Let me send a forwardable email to connect you two."


Review Materials: They can help you refine your pitch deck, financial model, and narrative.


Lend Credibility: They can put their face on your "Advisory Board" slide, which signals to investors that a respected industry veteran believes in your company.


Discuss Industry Trends: They can talk to investors generally about why the market needs your product.


What an Unlicensed Advisor CANNOT Do:


Negotiate Deal Terms: They cannot negotiate the valuation, cap, or interest rate on your SAFE or priced round with the investor.


Handle Funds: They cannot touch the investors' money or facilitate the actual transfer of funds.


Actively "Sell" the Securities: They shouldn't be the one pitching the specific financial merits of the investment. Their job is to make the introduction; your job (as the founders) is to pitch the deal and close it.


3. The "Finder" Exemption (A Gray Area to Avoid)


You might hear people talk about a "finder's fee." While some states have very narrow exemptions for "finders," at the federal level, the SEC is notoriously strict. If someone is consistently making introductions to investors and getting paid for it—even if they aren't negotiating the deal—the SEC may still classify them as an unregistered broker.


By using standard, time-vested advisory agreements (like the Founder Institute's FAST agreement), you avoid this gray area entirely. You are paying them for their overall strategic guidance, and their willingness to open their network to you is simply a byproduct of their belief in your company.


Structuring these agreements correctly protects both your cap table and your legal standing.

Here is the exact breakdown of how to structure the FAST agreement for these specific individuals.


1. The Equity Grant (The "How Much")
For an Expert-level advisor at the pre-seed stage, the standard FAST agreement dictates an equity grant of 0.50% to 1.0%.

2. The Vesting Schedule (The "How Long")
Unlike a Co-Founder who vests over 4 years, an advisor's maximum impact usually occurs in the first 12 to 24 months as they help you secure initial funding and launch the Go-To-Market strategy.


Standard FAST Vesting: 2 years (24 months).


Mechanics: The options vest in equal monthly increments (1/24th per month).


3. The Cliff (The Protection Mechanism)
Advisors are notorious for being highly enthusiastic in month one, and completely unresponsive by month four. The cliff is your legal protection against "dead equity."


Standard FAST Cliff: 3 Months.


Mechanics: If the advisor does not perform, stops answering emails, or fails to make the promised investor introductions, you terminate the agreement before the 90-day mark. They walk away with 0 shares. Upon hitting day 91, their first three months of equity vest simultaneously, and monthly vesting begins.


4. The Time Commitment & Duties
To ensure this remains a compliant advisory role (and not an unregistered broker-dealer arrangement), the agreement must quantify their compensation in terms of time and strategic support, not dollars raised.


Standard Commitment: 2 to 4 hours per month.


Defined Duties: * One 60-minute monthly strategic sync (reviewing the P&L, pitch deck, or GTM pipeline).


Asynchronous email support and reviewing materials.


Providing warm introductions to their professional network and prospective investors.


5. The "No Success Fee" Clause
While the FAST template is inherently compliant because it uses time-vested equity, you can add a simple clarifying sentence to the "Duties" section to legally bulletproof the document against SEC scrutiny:


"Advisor’s compensation is strictly for the strategic advisory services and time commitment outlined herein. Advisor is not acting as a placement agent, broker, or finder, and no transaction-based compensation or success fees shall be paid in connection with the raising of capital."


By offering a FAST agreement with a 3-month cliff, you give these high-level executives a standardized, professional path to join the team, while strictly protecting the company's equity and legal standing.
