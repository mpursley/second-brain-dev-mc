# Patio coffee bar

Replace the small table in the corner of the covered patio with a larger table
built as a coffee bar.

Tasks: [todo/life-stuff.md](../../todo/life-stuff.md) under House.

## What it has to do

Stated requirements, 2026-08-16:

- A **nice table top**
- A **mini fridge** for milk
- Room for the **coffee machine**, the **grinder**, and **cups**

Construction idea: **wrought iron pipe**. That is a preference, not a decision —
it implies building rather than buying.

## Target dimensions

Given 2026-08-16:

| | |
|---|---|
| Long | **60–64 in** |
| Tall | **38 in** |
| Deep | **24 in** |

**Unconfirmed:** whether these numbers came off the corner or are a target. The
corner has not been recorded as measured, so whether 64 in clears the door swing
is still unknown.

### What searching actually found

Checked against roughly 160 Amazon results on 2026-08-16
([source](../../raw/sessions/2026-08-16-coffee-bar-sourcing.md)).

**38 in does not exist in furniture.** Stock is 35.4 in (counter) or 41.3 in
(bar), with nothing between. That much was predicted.

**Depth is the harder constraint, and that was not predicted.** Console and bar
tables run 15.7–19.7 in deep because they are built to sit behind a couch. None
of them take a 24 in machine. Height and depth together rule out the whole
console / sideboard / buffet / bar-cabinet category.

**The category that fits is the garage workbench.** Natively 60 x 24, steel
frame, wood top, casters, and height sold as a specified feature. Same
industrial look as the mockups.

**Read the adjustment mechanism, not the range.** Most workbenches move in fixed
notches. A 60 x 24 Betterhood (`B0DNJXJN9V`, $219.99) has the ideal footprint
but nine levels running 25.5–37.5 in in 1.5 in steps — it tops out half an inch
short and cannot reach 38 at all.

## Candidates for the bar

Specs read off the product pages on 2026-08-16, not from search titles. Amazon's
`Product Dimensions` field is unreliable and its axis order is inconsistent; the
bullet text is usually the honest one. Prices and ratings are from that date and
will drift.

| [Homieasy island cart](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FY1MCYW3) `B0FY1MCYW3` | 20 x 39.4 x 35.3 | $149.99 | 4.5★ (46) |
| [TORACK workbench](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GWVC3RSW) `B0GWVC3RSW` | 23.5 x 60 x **29–41** | $199.99 | 4.4★ |
| [Betterhood workbench](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DNJXJN9V) `B0DNJXJN9V` | 24 x 60 x 25.5–37.5 | $219.99 | 4.4★ |
| [MUZIK 3-tier pipe legs](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09XDP2WWS) `B09XDP2WWS` | frame only | $29.99 | 4.1★ (167) |

**Homieasy** — has both the wheels and the pipe look. Real pipe legs, three
tiers, rustic brown, lockable casters already fitted; the closest thing found to
the mockup aesthetic. **But it is two-thirds the specified size** — 21–25 in
short on length, 4 in on depth, 2.7 in on height. The casters are already inside
the 35.3 in, so raising it is not simple. Engineered wood.

**Unconfirmed:** whether a mini fridge fits under its top shelf. Three tiers
inside 35.3 in leaves roughly 14–15 in of clearance and most mini fridges are
about 19 in tall. The actual shelf gap was not measured. If the fridge matters,
check this before buying.

**TORACK** — the only option that reaches 38 in cleanly. Height is set by
screwing the leg screws, so it is continuous rather than notched, and 38.0 is
just a setting. Powder-coated frame and solid rubberwood, 2000 lb — the most
weather-durable thing on this list, which matters out here. Two gaps against the
mockup: a thin 0.75 in top and no shelves at all. A butcher-block slab laid on
top fixes the look and adds about 1.5 in, so the legs would sit near 36.5 in.

**Betterhood** — listed only as the cautionary case. The 60 x 24 footprint is
exactly right, but its nine notches run 25.5–37.5 in in 1.5 in steps, so it
cannot reach 38 at all. Buying on the advertised range without reading the
mechanism is how this one gets bought by mistake.

**MUZIK pipe legs** — the wrought-iron-pipe route as a buyable part: a 3-tier
pipe frame, wood not included, so the top and shelves are chosen separately and
the height is whatever the pipe is cut to. **Treat its specs as unverified** —
the listing publishes no dimensions and its bullet text is recycled shoe-rack
copy, which suggests a repurposed listing. Cheap and on-style; ask the seller
for real dimensions before relying on it.

The **WLIVE Rustic Brown** is the one he picked out. Its **lift top is rated for

height. Worth a look, but check that before buying.

All four are engineered wood — see the durability note below.

## Two things that apply whatever is bought

**Engineered wood does not last outdoors here.** The Homieasy is MDF with a
printed woodgrain wrap. A photo from 2026-08-16 shows the patio is more open to
weather than the corner description suggests — string lights, a curtain, weather
reaching in from one side. In Bradenton humidity MDF swells at the edges and
delaminates once the wrap lifts. Not disqualifying at $150, but it is the
difference between furniture and a two-year purchase.

**Built-in power strips are indoor-rated.** Every workbench in this category
advertises one, and they are ETL-listed for dry indoor locations only. On an
open patio, running a fridge continuously, near a pool, that feature should not
be used. The current extension lead has the same defect. Solve power properly —
see the sequence below.

## Where it goes

The corner of the covered patio, against the stucco wall between the sliding
screen door and the french doors onto the pool. Currently a small bistro-height
metal table holding an electric hot plate, a moka pot, a utensil caddy, a mug,
and a cutting board — visibly more than it has room for.

Two things already live in that corner and are easy to overlook until the table
is built:

- **Power.** The current table is fed by an extension lead run along the floor
  to the doorway. A bar with a fridge and a machine on it wants a real circuit,
  and the fridge in particular runs continuously.
- **The pool robot.** It and its hose sit on the floor under and behind the
  table. A full-width console either displaces them or has to be planned around
  them.

A wider photo on 2026-08-16 showed the rest of the patio, which the corner-only
view had hidden. Past the french doors there is a **lounge end** — a black
daybed or futon with cushions and blankets, a low table in front of it, patio
chairs, and string lights strung overhead. He uses it with a laptop.

Two things follow. The patio is **more open to weather** than "covered patio"
implies — there is a curtain at one end and weather reaches in from the side,
which is what makes engineered wood a poor choice out here. And the coffee bar
is one end of a room that is already in use, not an isolated corner.

A lift-top coffee table for that end is being considered alongside the bar —
options under **Candidates for the lounge end** above.

## Design directions

Both from Gemini, both references rather than products. Nothing sourced or
priced. Images and full descriptions in
[the source](../../raw/patio/2026-08-16-coffee-bar.md).

**Industrial** — black square-tube frame, dark walnut top, two lower shelves for
mugs and syrup bottles, glass-fronted fridge in the right bay, towel bar on the
end.

**Steampunk** — same footprint in black iron pipe and fittings with a butcher
block top, plus a large copper hood canopy carrying gauges and an LED strip,
caged Edison lighting, and pipe-and-valve detailing along the front rail.

The steampunk version is the pipe idea taken all the way. The two share a
footprint and shelf layout, so the decision between them is finish and canopy,
not layout — which means the measuring and sizing work is the same either way
and can start before the style is settled.

**Neither mockup has wheels.** The industrial one has square-tube legs on flat
feet, the steampunk one pipe flanges. When he referred on 2026-08-16 to "the one
with the wheels" he meant the Homieasy cart above, not a mockup.

He does want it to roll, and noted that pipe legs are threaded at the bottom so
casters can always be added later. True for a build; not needed for the Homieasy,
which already rolls.

## Sequence

Roughly dependency-ordered rather than by effort:

1. **Measure the corner** and check the target above fits it — particularly
   whether 64 in clears the door swing.
2. **Settle the machine.** See below — this drives depth, power draw, and
   whether plumbing or a drip tray matters.
3. **Build or buy.** Pipe means build. A bought console means the canopy and
   detailing become separate add-ons.
4. **Power.** Solve properly rather than inheriting the extension lead.
5. **Top, fridge, shelving.** Sized from 1–3.
6. **Pool robot.** Needs a home either way.

## Open

**Which machine is this being built around?** The mockups show an espresso
machine with a portafilter and a burr grinder. The current photo shows a hot
plate and a moka pot. Whether that equipment is owned, wanted, or Gemini's
invention is **not established**, and it changes the bar substantially — a lever
espresso machine needs far more depth, weight tolerance, and power than a moka
pot on a hot plate.

**Is the size target firm, or was it aspirational?** This is the live question
as of 2026-08-16. The cart he likes the look of is 39.4 x 20 x 35.3; the target
he gave is 60–64 x 24 x 38. Both cannot be right. Either he would be happy with
the Homieasy's proportions, or it is a style reference and the search continues
at the specified size. **Measuring the corner settles it** — that is why the
measurement sits first in the sequence.

Also not established: budget, and whether this is a weekend build or a longer
project.
